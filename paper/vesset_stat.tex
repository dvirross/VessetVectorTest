\begin{center}{\bfseries Abstract}\end{center}

\noindent
Jewish religious law (\textit{Halacha}) prescribes that women anticipate menstruation
based on recurring patterns in their cycle history --- rules known as \textit{vesset}
(Hebrew: anticipated pattern of menstrual onset). Five of the 17 documented vesset
types are testable with cycle-length data: \textit{Haflaga} (fixed interval),
\textit{Dilug} (arithmetic progression), \textit{Week} (weekly anchor),
\textit{Week-Dilug} (30-day anchor), and \textit{Dilug-in-Dilug} (second-order
progression). These form a polynomial hierarchy whose structure generates mathematically
necessary dependencies, confirmed by pairwise permutation tests ($p_{\text{perm}} < .014$).
We apply three complementary null models to 1,554 menstrual cycles from 118 women
\cite{fehring2013}: a global permutation null, a multinomial sampling null, and a
within-woman permutation null conditioning on each woman's observed
cycle-length multiset. Under the global null, \textit{Haflaga} is strongly
overrepresented ($z = 4.51$, $p < .001$; Mahalanobis joint test $p < .001$),
reflecting concentration of patterns among intrinsically regular women.
Under the within-woman null --- the scientifically sharper test --- no pattern
is significant (all $p \geq .722$; joint $D_M = 2.95$, $p = .121$), and all observed
counts fall \emph{below} their within-woman null means. These complementary results
establish that halachic vesset rules function primarily as empirical detectors of
individual cycle regularity, rather than as rules exploiting serial temporal
dependence beyond that regularity.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Keywords:} menstrual cycle, vesset, Halacha, permutation test,
within-woman permutation, multinomial sampling, natural family planning, Jewish law,
polynomial hierarchy, dependence structure, multivariate randomization test

\section{Introduction}

Menstrual cycles exhibit substantial variability both within and across individuals
\cite{munster1992,creinin2004,symul2019}. The cycle length distribution is
approximately bell-shaped with a pronounced right tail (skewness $\approx 1.3$ in the
present dataset), reflecting a small proportion of unusually long cycles. Within-person
consistency is moderate: intraclass correlation coefficients for cycle length are
typically in the range of $0.3$--$0.5$ \cite{dasharathy2012}, indicating meaningful
regularity beneath the surface variability. Bayesian hierarchical state-space models have
been proposed to exploit this temporal structure for cycle-length prediction
\cite{bortot2010}, confirming that within-woman dynamics carry genuine predictive
information.

In the Jewish religious tradition, this regularity is also subject to detailed legal
analysis. Halachic law (\textit{Halacha}, Jewish religious law) requires that a woman anticipate the onset of
her next menstruation based on patterns discernible in her prior cycle history. These
anticipatory rules are called \textit{vesset}, and they govern a system of abstinence
obligations central to the observance of \textit{taharat hamishpacha} (family purity: the traditional Jewish
system of laws governing marital intimacy in relation to the menstrual cycle). Of the 17 vesset pattern types documented in halachic literature, 10 require
date or time-of-day data unavailable in standard cycle-length records; the remaining
7 are testable empirically. Two of these 7 (Dilug Chozer Chalila and Haflaga Chozer Chalila
(Hebrew: repeating-cycle arithmetic progression and repeating fixed interval,
respectively) occurred zero times in the present dataset and are not reported separately.
The five patterns with positive observed frequencies are analysed here.

These five patterns form a \textit{polynomial hierarchy}: each describes a \textit{haflaga} (Hebrew: inter-menstrual interval, $H_k = L_k + 1$)
sequence that follows a polynomial of a specific degree in cycle index. Haflaga,
Week, and Week-Dilug are all degree-0 (constant) sequences; Dilug is degree-1
(linear); Dilug-in-Dilug is degree-2 (quadratic). This hierarchy generates
mathematically necessary dependence relationships between the patterns, with
implications for how their joint frequencies should be tested.

Despite the centuries-long practical application of vesset rules --- and the modern
availability of large longitudinal cycle datasets --- no published study has formally
tested whether these patterns occur in empirical data at rates exceeding chance. This
is a non-trivial question: if vesset rules merely detect random fluctuations, their
anticipatory validity is weakened. Conversely, if they are significantly
overrepresented, their empirical basis is independently corroborated.

The present study addresses this gap by applying two complementary null models ---
a permutation test \cite{edgington2007} and a multinomial sampling null
\cite{ross2022phd} --- to a longitudinal NFP dataset \cite{fehring2013}. We derive
per-pattern p-values, effect sizes, and a joint multivariate test that accommodates
the dependence structure among pattern counts without assuming independence. The joint
test is an original methodological contribution \cite{ross2022phd}, inspired by the
simulation-based testing philosophy of King et al.\ \cite{king2018} though distinct
from their density-based approach; a formal theoretical treatment is planned in future
work. We also characterise the mathematical dependence structure through pairwise
chi-square tests and derive the dependencies analytically from the pattern definitions.

This study extends a line of quantitative halachic scholarship \cite{ross2022markov,
ross2019reliability,ross2021statistical}. To our knowledge it is the first to test vesset pattern frequencies against
an explicit randomisation null, though we note this claim has not been verified
by a systematic literature search.

% ─── 2. Background ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Background}

\subsection{The Polynomial Hierarchy of Vesset Rules}

Let $L_1, L_2, \ldots, L_n$ denote the menstrual cycle lengths (in days) of a single
woman, and define the \textit{haflaga} $H_k = L_k + 1$ (the interval from the first
day of menstruation $k$ to the first day of menstruation $k+1$, following halachic
convention \cite{ross2021statistical}). The five vesset pattern types are:

\begin{description}
  \item[\textit{Week-Dilug.}] $H_k = H_{k-1} = 30$ (two or more consecutive haflagot
    both equal to 30). Degree-0 polynomial (constant) at the canonical 30-day anchor.
    Minimum run: 2 cycles.

  \item[\textit{Week.}] $H_k = H_{k-1}$, $H_k \in \mathcal{W}$ where
    $\mathcal{W} = \{H_{\min}+3, H_{\min}+10, \ldots\}$ (values spaced 7 apart).
    Degree-0 polynomial anchored to weekly values. Minimum run: 2 cycles.

  \item[\textit{Haflaga.}] $H_k = H_{k-1} = H_{k-2}$ (three or more consecutive
    equal haflagot, any value). Degree-0 polynomial (constant, unrestricted).
    Minimum run: 3 cycles.

  \item[\textit{Dilug.}] $H_k - H_{k-1} = H_{k-1} - H_{k-2} = d \neq 0$ (constant
    non-zero first difference). Degree-1 polynomial (linear).
    Minimum run: 3 cycles.

  \item[\textit{Dilug-in-Dilug.}] $(H_k - H_{k-1}) - (H_{k-1} - H_{k-2}) =
    (H_{k-1} - H_{k-2}) - (H_{k-2} - H_{k-3}) = d \neq 0$ (constant non-zero second
    difference). Degree-2 polynomial (quadratic). Minimum run: 4 cycles.
\end{description}

The hierarchy Haflaga (degree 0) $\to$ Dilug (degree 1) $\to$ Dilug-in-Dilug
(degree 2) mirrors the classical Taylor expansion: a constant function is the
zeroth-order approximation, a linear function the first-order, and a quadratic the
second-order.

\subsection{Mathematical Dependence Structure}

The polynomial hierarchy generates three analytically derivable dependence
relationships, which we verify empirically in Section~\ref{sec:chisq}:

\paragraph{Haflaga and Week-Dilug --- set-theoretic inclusion.}
Week-Dilug requires $H_k = H_{k-1} = 30$; Haflaga-30 requires $H_k = H_{k-1} =
H_{k-2} = 30$. Therefore:
\[
  \{\text{Haflaga-30 sequence}\} \;\subseteq\; \{\text{Week-Dilug sequence}\}.
\]
Every Haflaga sequence with value 30 automatically generates a Week-Dilug event in
its first two cycles. This is set-theoretic inclusion, not merely statistical
correlation. In the present data, 3 of the 26 observed Haflaga sequences have value
$H = 30$, each contributing directly to the Week-Dilug count of 18.

\paragraph{Haflaga and Dilug-in-Dilug --- proximity in the hierarchy.}
When the second difference $|d|$ of a Dilug-in-Dilug sequence is small, its first
differences are nearly equal and the haflaga sequence is nearly constant --- i.e.\
nearly a Haflaga. In the data, 16 of 27 observed DiD sequences have $|d| = 1$,
making them almost indistinguishable from a constant sequence. Both patterns further
share the same structural prerequisite: a minimum run of $\geq 3$ consecutive cycles
satisfying a regularity condition, selecting the same women with high within-person
cycle stability (within-person SD: Haflaga women $= 1.54$ days vs.\ no-vesset women
$= 3.17$ days).

\paragraph{Dilug and Week-Dilug --- logical incompatibility.}
Dilug requires $\Delta H_k \neq 0$ (strictly non-constant differences); Week-Dilug
requires $H_k = H_{k-1} = 30$ ($\Delta H = 0$). These conditions are mutually
exclusive within the same cycle window, creating a negative dependence tendency.

\subsection{Prior Work}

\paragraph{Menstrual cycle statistics.}
Variability and regularity of cycle length have been extensively characterised
\cite{munster1992,creinin2004,dasharathy2012,symul2019}. Bortot et al.\
\cite{bortot2010} developed a Bayesian hierarchical state-space model for sequential
prediction of cycle lengths, exploiting within-woman temporal dependence; their model
provides a benchmark for cycle-length predictability.

\paragraph{Quantitative halachic analysis.}
Ross \cite{ross2022markov} modelled cycle transitions with Markov chains;
\cite{ross2019reliability} evaluated reliability of self-reported menstrual data in
a halachic context; \cite{ross2021statistical} applied statistical analysis to
abstinence laws. The present study adds the first formal hypothesis test of whether
vesset patterns are overrepresented in real data.

\paragraph{Randomization-based testing.}
Permutation tests \cite{edgington2007,good2005} provide exact finite-sample p-values
under exchangeability. The multinomial sampling null \cite{ross2022phd} draws each
woman's cycles independently from the fitted empirical distribution, directly encoding
the null hypothesis of cycle independence. For the joint test, King et al.\
\cite{king2018} proposed a general framework for testing a vector of statistics via
simulation of the joint density, using a multivariate kernel density estimator to
define a smallest-acceptance-region test. The present joint test differs: we use the
Euclidean distance from the null mean vector as the test statistic, which is simpler,
requires no density estimation, and has an intuitive geometric interpretation. A formal
theoretical comparison and analysis of the distance-based approach is planned in future
work \cite{ross2022phd}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

We analyzed a publicly available longitudinal dataset originally collected by Fehring
et al.\ \cite{fehring2013} in a randomised trial comparing two internet-supported
natural family planning methods, archived at Marquette University
\cite{marquette2013data}. After excluding cycles shorter than 18 days or longer than
54 days \cite{creinin2004}, the final sample comprised \textbf{1,554 cycles from 118
women} ($M = 13.2$ cycles per woman, $\text{SD} = 7.0$, range $1$--$45$).

Raw cycle lengths ranged from 18 to 54 days ($M = 29.3$, $\text{SD} = 3.9$,
$\text{Mdn} = 28.5$, skewness $= 1.29$). The distribution is approximately
bell-shaped with a right tail, consistent with the literature
\cite{munster1992,creinin2004}. Women ranged in age from 21 to 43 years
($M = 30.6$, $\text{SD} = 5.7$). The dataset originates from two charting cohorts
($n_0 = 92$ and $n_1 = 67$ women in the raw data prior to exclusions;
$n_0 = 69$ and $n_1 = 49$ women in the filtered sample). All cycle lengths were incremented by 1 to conform to the
halachic haflaga convention ($H_k = L_k + 1$), yielding $M = 30.3$, $\text{SD} = 3.9$.

The within-person variability structure is relevant to the Haflaga result: women who
establish a Haflaga vesset have a mean within-person cycle-length SD of 1.54 days,
compared to 3.17 days for women who establish no vesset. This confirms that Haflaga
selects the most physiologically regular subgroup of women. Figure~\ref{fig:dist}
shows the cycle length distribution and per-woman variability.

\subsection{Pattern Detection}

Pattern counting was performed within each woman's sequence independently; sequences
were never merged across women. Patient boundary pairs $(s_i, f_i)$ were encoded
explicitly and all counting loops were constrained to $[s_i, f_i]$. Extended runs of
the same pattern were counted as a single occurrence to avoid double-counting (e.g.,
four consecutive equal haflagot constitute one Haflaga, not two). All five functions
were validated against manually annotated examples and are available at
\url{https://github.com/dvirross/VessetVectorTest}.

\subsection{Null Models}
\label{sec:nullmodels}

Three null models address distinct scientific questions:
\begin{description}
  \item[$H_G$ \textnormal{(global exchangeability):}] All 1,554 cycle lengths exchangeable
    across women. Tests whether patterns exceed population-level chance.
  \item[$H_W$ \textnormal{(within-woman permutation):}] Each woman\'s cycles permuted uniformly at random within her own sequence (without replacement). Tests whether temporal \emph{ordering} creates excess
    patterns beyond individual regularity.
  \item[$H_{iid}$ \textnormal{(multinomial i.i.d.):}] Each woman\'s cycles drawn i.i.d.
    from $\hat{F}$. Stronger than $H_G$: replaces observed values, not just ordering.
\end{description}

$H_G$ and $H_{iid}$ are the global nulls (permutation and multinomial respectively);
$H_W$ is the within-woman null. All three were run for $N = 50{,}000$ iterations (seed 17).

\paragraph{Permutation test.}
At each iteration, the $n = 1{,}554$ haflaga values are randomly permuted without
replacement. This preserves the exact empirical marginal distribution and is
equivalent to assuming all $n!$ orderings are equally probable under $H_0$
\cite{edgington2007}.

\paragraph{Multinomial null.}
At each iteration, for each woman $i$ with $n_i$ cycles, a fresh sequence
$\tilde{H}_{i,1}, \ldots, \tilde{H}_{i,n_i} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \hat{F}$
is drawn, where $\hat{F}$ is the empirical CDF of all 1,554 observed haflaga values.
This null directly encodes the hypothesis that cycles are \textit{independent} across
time within each woman \cite{ross2022phd}. A naive bootstrap (global resampling with
replacement) is structurally equivalent given the fixed per-woman boundary structure
and is therefore not reported separately.

\subsection{Statistical Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

For each pattern $j$ and null model $m$ we computed: the observed count $O_j$; the
null mean $\bar{S}_j^{(m)}$ and SD $\sigma_j^{(m)}$; the z-score $z_j^{(m)} =
(O_j - \bar{S}_j^{(m)}) / \sigma_j^{(m)}$; and the one-sided empirical p-value
$p_j^{(m)} = (b_j+1)/(B+1)$, where $b_j = \#\{S_{j,r}^{(m)} \geq O_j\}$ and $B = 50{,}000$, following the Monte Carlo convention of Davison \& Hinkley~\cite{davison1997}.

The \textbf{joint multivariate test} uses the Euclidean distance of the observed
count vector from the null mean vector as the test statistic:
\begin{equation}
  D^{(m)} = \bigl\|\mathbf{O} - \bar{\mathbf{S}}^{(m)}\bigr\|_2
  = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{5}\bigl(O_j - \bar{S}_j^{(m)}\bigr)^2},
  \label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}
with p-value equal to the fraction of iterations where the simulated distance exceeded
$D^{(m)}$. Unlike Bonferroni--Holm correction --- which, while valid under arbitrary
dependence, is conservative when tests are positively correlated --- this joint permutation test operates on the full count vector simultaneously,
respecting the dependence structure. Note that this is a single global test of
the multivariate null, not an FWER procedure across the five component tests. Inspired by King et al.\ \cite{king2018} but using distance rather
than density as the test statistic, this approach is, to our knowledge, the first
application of a distance-based joint permutation test to sequentially-defined
pattern count vectors in longitudinal medical data. We adopt the \textbf{Mahalanobis distance} $D_M^2 = (\mathbf{O}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{O}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$ as the primary statistic, as it correctly standardises each component by its null variance and accounts for inter-component correlations. Non-diagonal terms in $\hat{\Sigma}$ are expected a priori: the set-theoretic containment of Week-Dilug within Haflaga-30 and the polynomial proximity of Dilug-in-Dilug to Haflaga (Section~\ref{sec:mathdep}) imply that the five pattern counts are not marginally independent, so a diagonal (Euclidean) standardisation would misrepresent the dependence structure. $\hat{\Sigma}^{(m)}$ is estimated empirically from the $B = 50{,}000$ simulated count vectors under each null model, yielding a $5 \times 5$ sample covariance matrix. Condition numbers of 4.4 (permutation null) and 5.4 (multinomial null) confirm that the matrix is well-conditioned and the inversion is numerically stable. The Euclidean distance $D$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:distance}) is retained as a secondary statistic for geometric interpretability.

A \textbf{focused joint test} restricted to the three mathematically dependent
patterns (Haflaga, Week-Dilug, Dilug-in-Dilug) is reported in Section~\ref{sec:chisq}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity.}
The joint test is sensitive primarily to the Haflaga component, which contributes
the largest standardised excess ($z = 4.51$) and --- after variance standardisation
by the Mahalanobis metric --- the largest weight. Under the global permutation null,
the observed $D_M = 5.10$ exceeds the 99.9th percentile of the null distribution.

Pairwise \textbf{chi-square tests of independence} between pattern types were computed
on the per-woman binary matrix (1 = woman established the pattern at least once).
$\alpha = .05$ throughout.

\paragraph{Simulation pipeline.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sim} provides a formal pseudocode description of the full
simulation pipeline for reproducibility.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Full simulation pipeline}
\label{alg:sim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(H_{i,1},\ldots,H_{i,n_i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ ($N$ women),
         patterns $j = 1,\ldots,5$, iterations $B$, seed
\State Compute observed counts $\mathbf{O} = (O_1,\ldots,O_5)$ via pattern detection
\For{$r = 1$ to $B$}
  \State // Global permutation null ($H_G$): shuffle all $\sum_i n_i$ haflaga values
  \State $\mathbf{H}^{(G)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Permute}(\bigcup_i \{H_{i,k}\})$;
         re-assign to women preserving $n_i$
  \State $\mathbf{S}^{(G,r)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Count}(\mathbf{H}^{(G)})$
  \State // Multinomial null ($H_{iid}$): draw each $H_{i,k}$ iid from marginal distribution
  \State $\mathbf{H}^{(M)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Multinomial}(p_1,\ldots,p_K; \text{total } \textstyle\sum_i n_i)$
  \State $\mathbf{S}^{(M,r)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Count}(\mathbf{H}^{(M)})$
  \State // Within-woman permutation null ($H_W$): shuffle within each woman
  \For{$i = 1$ to $N$}
    \State $\mathbf{H}^{(W)}_i \leftarrow \operatorname{Permute}(H_{i,1},\ldots,H_{i,n_i})$
           \Comment{without replacement}
  \EndFor
  \State $\mathbf{S}^{(W,r)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Count}(\mathbf{H}^{(W)})$
\EndFor
\State Estimate $\hat{\Sigma}^{(m)} \leftarrow \operatorname{SampleCov}(\{\mathbf{S}^{(m,r)}\}_{r=1}^{B})$
       for each $m \in \{G, M, W\}$
\For{$m \in \{G, M, W\}$}
  \State Null mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)} \leftarrow B^{-1}\sum_r \mathbf{S}^{(m,r)}$
  \State Mahalanobis statistic:
         $D_M^{(m)} \leftarrow \sqrt{(\mathbf{O}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)})^\top
         (\hat{\Sigma}^{(m)})^{-1}(\mathbf{O}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)})}$
  \State $p_j^{(m)} \leftarrow (|\{r : S_j^{(m,r)} \geq O_j\}|+1)/(B+1)$
         for each pattern $j$ \Comment{one-sided}
  \State $p_{D_M}^{(m)} \leftarrow (|\{r : D_M^{(m,r)} \geq D_M^{(m)}|+1)/(B+1)$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Within-Woman Permutation Analysis}
\label{sec:stratified}

The global permutation and multinomial null models permute or resample haflaga values
\emph{across} all 118 women, thereby removing between-woman heterogeneity in cycle
regularity. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:nullmodels}, this means both global nulls
test whether vesset patterns are more frequent than expected if all women drew from
the same population distribution --- not whether patterns are more frequent than
expected \emph{given each woman's own level of regularity}. These are distinct
scientific questions.

To address the within-woman question, we implemented a \textbf{within-woman permutation} null ($H_W$): for each woman independently, her haflaga sequence is randomly permuted (shuffled without replacement), preserving her individual cycle-length \emph{multiset} while randomising the temporal ordering. This null encodes the hypothesis that the
\emph{temporal ordering} of cycles within a woman does not create patterns beyond what her
own cycle-length values (taken in random order) would predict.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
For each of the 118 women independently, her haflaga sequence is permuted uniformly at random (without replacement). All women are included with no exclusion threshold. The aggregate null is generated by summing the permuted count vectors across all 118 women, producing $B = 50{,}000$ realisations of the joint count vector.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The two nulls answer different scientific questions and are both valid:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{global null} tests: \emph{are vesset patterns more frequent than
    expected if all cycle lengths were exchangeable across all women?} The answer is
    yes for Haflaga ($p < .001$ after Holm correction), reflecting that vesset patterns
    are concentrated among women who are intrinsically more regular.
  \item The \textbf{within-woman null} tests: \emph{given each woman's own level of
    cycle regularity, are the temporal arrangements unusually structured?} The answer
    is no --- patterns arise naturally from individual cycle regularity without
    additional temporal dependence.
\end{itemize}
Together, these results suggest that halachic vesset rules --- particularly Haflaga
--- constitute an empirical codification of individual menstrual regularity: the rules
correctly identify the subset of women whose cycles are intrinsically stable, and
formalise that stability as an anticipatory pattern. Whether this reflects deliberate
halachic wisdom or fortuitous alignment with biology is beyond the scope of the
present analysis. A companion study examining the practical forecasting efficiency of halachic
vesset rules --- how accurately they predict the next menstruation onset, and\nat what cost in unnecessary abstinence days --- is in preparation \cite{ross_planned}.

\section{Discussion}

This study provides the first formal statistical test of whether halachic vesset
patterns are overrepresented in empirical menstrual cycle data. Using $N = 50{,}000$
iterations of permutation and multinomial null models, we found strong and consistent
evidence for two of the five patterns analysed: \textit{Haflaga} ($z = 4.51$,
$p < .001$) and \textit{Dilug} ($z = 1.83$, $p = .043$). The joint multivariate test
confirmed significant displacement of the observed count vector from the null distribution ($D_M = 5.10$, $p < .001$), driven primarily by the Haflaga component; the focused test on the three mathematically dependent patterns (Haflaga, Week-Dilug, Dilug-in-Dilug) was similarly significant ($p < .01$).

\subsection{Haflaga}

The Haflaga finding is the most striking result. With only 2 of 50,000 permutation
simulations reaching or exceeding the observed count of 26 (the simulation ceiling),
the empirical evidence is essentially unambiguous. The result is biologically
interpretable: Haflaga detects exactly the pattern expected in women with high
within-person cycle regularity. The present data confirm this: women who establish
Haflaga have within-person SDs of 1.54 days vs.\ 3.17 days for women who establish no
vesset at all. These findings are consistent with the interpretation that Haflaga formalises an empirically detectable physiological regularity in cycle timing.

\subsection{Dilug}

The Dilug finding ($p = .043$) is more modest but consistent across both null models.
Arithmetic progressions in cycle length may arise from gradual physiological trends
within a woman's cycle history, e.g., gradual lengthening or shortening associated
with hormonal change, ageing, or weight change. The significance of Dilug suggests
that such directional trends are more common than random arrangements would produce,
warranting replication in larger samples. These biological explanations are speculative and await independent confirmation in datasets with longitudinal physiological measurements.

\subsection{Mathematical Dependence Structure as a Unifying Observation}

A distinctive feature of this study is that the observed statistical dependences between
pattern types are not post-hoc empirical discoveries but \emph{mathematical necessities}
derivable from the pattern definitions alone.  This is, to our knowledge, an observation
that has not previously been stated explicitly in the halachic or statistical literature.

\paragraph{The finite-difference hierarchy.}
The five vesset patterns are naturally unified by the language of finite differences.
Recall that the $n$-th forward difference operator $\Delta^n$ is defined recursively by
$\Delta^0 H_k = H_k$ and $\Delta^n H_k = \Delta^{n-1}H_{k+1} - \Delta^{n-1}H_k$.
Each vesset pattern corresponds to a condition on a specific order of $\Delta$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Pattern & Condition & Order \\
\midrule
Week-Dilug & $H_k = 30$ (constant at 30) & $\Delta^0 H_k = 30$ \\
Week       & $H_k = c \in \mathcal{W}$ (constant, anchored) & $\Delta^0 H_k = c$ \\
Haflaga    & $H_k = c$ (constant, any $c$) & $\Delta^0 H_k = c$ \\
Dilug      & $\Delta^1 H_k = d \neq 0$ (constant non-zero first difference) & \\
Dilug-in-Dilug & $\Delta^2 H_k = d \neq 0$ (constant non-zero second difference) & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
This ordering mirrors the classical Taylor expansion: a constant function is a
degree-0 polynomial, a linear function is degree-1, and a quadratic is degree-2.
Haflaga, Week, and Week-Dilug detect degree-0 sequences (with progressively tighter
constraints on which constant value is admissible); Dilug detects degree-1 sequences
($H_k = a + bk$, $b \neq 0$); and Dilug-in-Dilug detects degree-2 sequences
($H_k = a + bk + ck^2$, $c \neq 0$).  Crucially, a degree-2 sequence with a very
small leading coefficient $c$ is nearly linear, and a degree-1 sequence with a very
small slope $b$ is nearly constant --- so the hierarchy does not impose rigid
boundaries; rather, it describes a spectrum of regularity along which the patterns
sit at adjacent levels.

This graded structure generates three mathematically necessary relationships between
the patterns.

\paragraph{Haflaga and Week-Dilug: containment.}
Week-Dilug requires two consecutive \textit{haflagot} (pl.\ of \textit{haflaga}) both equal to 30, i.e.\
$\Delta^0 H_{k-1} = \Delta^0 H_k = 30$.  Haflaga-30 requires three consecutive
haflagot all equal to 30, i.e.\ $\Delta^0 H_{k-2} = \Delta^0 H_{k-1} = \Delta^0
H_k = 30$, which strictly contains the Week-Dilug condition.  Algebraically,
\[
  \bigl\{H_{k-2} = H_{k-1} = H_k = 30\bigr\}
  \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
  \bigl\{H_{k-1} = H_k = 30\bigr\},
\]
so every Haflaga-30 sequence necessarily generates a Week-Dilug event at the same
position.  This is not a probabilistic statement but a deterministic logical
implication: any haflaga sequence that satisfies the Haflaga-30 condition
automatically satisfies the Week-Dilug condition at its first two elements.  In
the data, 3 of the 26 Haflaga sequences have value $H = 30$ and therefore each
count once towards the Week-Dilug total as a direct algebraic consequence.

\paragraph{Haflaga and Dilug-in-Dilug: limiting convergence.}
A Dilug-in-Dilug sequence with second difference $d$ has first differences
$\Delta^1 H_k = b,\, b+d,\, b+2d,\, \ldots$, so the haflaga values are
\[
  H_k = H_0 + bk + \tfrac{d}{2}k(k-1).
\]
As $d \to 0$, this reduces to $H_k = H_0 + bk$ (a Dilug), and as both $d \to 0$
and $b \to 0$, it reduces to $H_k = H_0$ (a Haflaga).  More precisely, for a run
of length $n$ starting at $H_0$, the maximum deviation from the constant sequence
$\mathbf{c} = H_0$ is bounded by
\[
  \|H - \mathbf{c}\|_\infty \;\leq\; |b|\,n + \tfrac{|d|}{2}\,n^2.
\]
A DiD sequence with $|d| = 1$ and a short run ($n = 4$, the minimum) therefore
deviates from the nearest constant by at most $4|b| + 8$ days --- for small $|b|$,
this is a sequence that is barely distinguishable from a Haflaga.  In the data,
16 of the 27 observed DiD sequences have $|d| = 1$, the minimum non-zero value;
for these, the haflaga values change by only 1 day per cycle relative to linear
trend.  The women who sustain a DiD pattern with $|d| = 1$ are the same women whose
cycles are regular enough to sustain a near-constant sequence across multiple cycles
--- women with a similar degree of cycle regularity to Haflaga women.  This explains the strong
positive dependence ($\chi^2 = 9.99$, $p_{\text{perm}} = .004$): the finite-difference hierarchy places
Haflaga and DiD at adjacent levels of polynomial order, and the data show that real
women tend to cluster near the boundary between them.

\paragraph{Dilug and Week-Dilug: mutual exclusion.}
Dilug requires $\Delta^1 H_k = H_k - H_{k-1} = d \neq 0$; Week-Dilug requires
$\Delta^1 H_k = H_k - H_{k-1} = 0$.  Within any single cycle window $(k-1, k)$,
these conditions are contradictory:
\[
  \Delta^1 H_k \neq 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \Delta^1 H_k = 0
\]
cannot both hold.  A cycle pair that satisfies Week-Dilug ($\Delta^1 H = 0$) is
therefore excluded from contributing to a Dilug run ($\Delta^1 H \neq 0$), and vice
versa.  This mutual exclusion at the window level predicts a negative co-occurrence
tendency at the woman level.  Although the pair did not reach significance in our
sample ($\chi^2 = 1.46$, $p_{\text{perm}} = .300$), the direction is negative (5 observed
co-occurrences vs.\ 7.3 expected), consistent with the prediction from the algebra.

\paragraph{Implication for inference.}
The three relationships derived above --- containment, limiting convergence, and
mutual exclusion --- show that the five pattern counts are \emph{structurally}
non-independent: their joint distribution is constrained by the algebra of
finite differences, not by coincidence.  Applying Bonferroni--Holm correction controls the family-wise error rate (FWER) under
arbitrary dependence and does not require independence. However, it is known to
be conservative when tests are positively correlated, as is the case here.
The joint permutation test evaluates the count vector as a single entity, avoiding any multiple-comparison correction and sidestepping the conservatism of Holm--Bonferroni under positive dependence.  The joint
multivariate test of Section~\ref{sec:inference} operates
directly on the count vector, without any independence assumption, thereby respecting
the dependence structure that the finite-difference hierarchy imposes.

\subsection{The Joint Multivariate Test}

The joint test (Eq.~\ref{eq:distance}) is an original methodological contribution
\cite{ross2022phd}. We adopt the \textbf{Mahalanobis distance} as the primary
test statistic after confirming that the null covariance matrices are well-conditioned
(condition numbers 4.4--5.4; all pairwise correlations $|r| < 0.16$, so inversion is
numerically stable). The Mahalanobis distance correctly standardises each component
by its null variance --- making a 1-unit excess in Haflaga (null SD $= 3.26$)
appropriately more influential than a 1-unit excess in Dilug (null SD $= 6.60$) ---
and is the natural multivariate generalisation of the univariate z-score.

The result is striking: $D_M = 5.10$ ($p < .001$) under the permutation null and
$D_M = 4.84$ ($p < .001$) under the multinomial null. These yield approximately 100-fold smaller p-values than the Euclidean counterparts ($p = .009$ and $.014$),
because Haflaga --- with null SD $= 3.26$, the smallest of the five --- receives
its correctly up-weighted contribution. More precisely, $D_M^2 = (\mathbf{O} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{O} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$ standardises deviations by the full null covariance matrix; in the approximately diagonal case, this reduces to $\sum_j (O_j - \mu_j)^2/\sigma_j^2$, so components with smaller null variance receive proportionally greater weight. Haflaga has the smallest null SD of the five (3.26 vs.\ 6.60 for Dilug), so its deviation of $(26 - 11.30) = 14.7$ units receives the largest standardised contribution.

This approach is inspired by King et al.\ \cite{king2018} and related to
energy-distance tests \cite{szekely2013} and multivariate permutation tests
\cite{pesarin2010}, but differs in using Mahalanobis distance from the simulated
null mean --- requiring no density estimation, pairwise distances, or asymptotic
approximations. The p-value is purely empirical. A formal theoretical treatment is
planned in future work.

\paragraph{Relationship to Hotelling's $T^2$.}
The permutation-based Mahalanobis test is related to, but more general than,
Hotelling's $T^2$. Under a Gaussian approximation, $D_M^2$ is asymptotically
$\chi^2(5)$, which is the same distributional assumption underlying $T^2$; however,
the exact permutation reference distribution used here replaces that parametric
assumption with an empirical one, making the test distribution-free. Hotelling's $T^2$
is an exact test under multivariate Gaussian sampling but requires this distributional
assumption for validity; the permutation reference distribution does not. Moreover, the
Bonferroni--Holm correction applied to the five marginal tests is provably valid but
conservative under positive correlation (as holds here via the set-theoretic
containment and polynomial proximity relationships), while the joint Mahalanobis test
exploits the known positive correlation structure by pooling the five components
simultaneously. For both reasons --- distribution-free inference and proper treatment
of dependence --- the permutation-based Mahalanobis test is the preferred approach
for this application.

\subsection{Non-Significant Patterns}

Week, Week-Dilug, and Dilug-in-Dilug were not individually significant. These results
do not invalidate these halachic rules, which are established on legal-theological
grounds \cite{ross2021statistical}. The non-significance of Dilug-in-Dilug is
particularly notable given its large z-score at the sequence level in the joint test:
it is the dependence with Haflaga (via the polynomial hierarchy) that elevates its
collective contribution to $D$, even when its individual count is exactly at the null
mean. This illustrates why joint and individual tests can differ.

For the Week pattern, it is worth noting that the weekly anchor set
$\mathcal{W} = \{22, 29, 36, 43, 50\}$ (in haflaga units) corresponds to cycle lengths
$\{21, 28, 35, 42, 49\}$. Since cycles of 35 days or longer are well outside the
normal range for the Fehring dataset (mean $L = 29.3$, SD $= 3.9$), nearly all
Week patterns in this sample arise from haflagot of 22 (cycle length 21) or 29
(cycle length 28). The 28-day cycle is the mode of the dataset and the most
biologically common length, so the Week pattern in this dataset effectively captures
women with near-28-day cycles who also show the requisite two-consecutive-cycle
consistency.

\subsection{Medical and Clinical Relevance}

The findings have several implications for reproductive health and fertility awareness,
even within the scope of this dataset and without the companion forecasting study.

\paragraph{Fertility awareness and achieving pregnancy.}
Accurate anticipation of the next menstruation onset allows backward estimation of the
fertile window (approximately 14 days prior). Women with established Haflaga --- identified
by the present analysis as the most intrinsically regular subgroup (within-person SD:
1.54 days vs.\ 3.17 days for non-vesset women) --- are the prime candidates for
calendar-based fertile window estimation, since their cycle regularity makes such
estimates most reliable. This is relevant for both natural family planning for pregnancy
achievement and for infertility management, where timing of intercourse relative to
the fertile window is critical \cite{bortot2010}.

\paragraph{Halachic family purity compliance.}
For observant Jewish women, the vesset system governs the timing of ritual abstinence
before the anticipated menstrual onset (\textit{taharat hamishpacha}). Accurate
anticipation reduces unnecessary abstinence days while fulfilling the halachic obligation.
In the present dataset (non-Jewish NFP users as a proxy sample), vesset-based forecasting
yielded 451 correct anticipations out of 3,415 total forecasting opportunities (13.2\%),
with a 2.49:1 ratio of unnecessary abstinences to correct anticipations \cite{ross2022phd}.
The companion study in preparation \cite{ross_planned} will quantify whether this ratio improves for the Haflaga-established subgroup and how it compares to modern statistical forecasting methods.

\paragraph{Cycle regularity monitoring and health surveillance.}
Menstrual cycle irregularity is associated with decreased fertility and increased risk
of chronic conditions including breast cancer, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes
\cite{bortot2010}. The present finding that Haflaga selects for women with within-person
SD below 1.6 days suggests it could serve as a simple rule-based screen for cycle
regularity --- potentially useful as a low-cost indicator of reproductive health status
in settings where laboratory or hormonal assessment is unavailable.

\paragraph{Detecting change in individual regularity.}
The within-woman null finding --- that patterns arise from individual regularity rather
than additional temporal structure --- implies a useful clinical corollary: a change in
vesset status (failure to re-establish a previously established Haflaga, or establishment
of a new pattern after a period of irregularity) could serve as a sensitive indicator
of disruption to baseline regularity, whether from hormonal change, weight change,
stress, or perimenopausal transition. This is a clinical hypothesis for future study.

\paragraph{Digital health and cycle-tracking applications.}
Menstrual cycle tracking apps are now widely used for fertility awareness and cycle
monitoring \cite{symul2019}. Vesset rules, as formalised pattern-detection algorithms
operating on cycle-length sequences, could be implemented in such applications to
automatically classify women by cycle regularity type, flag pattern establishment or
breakdown, and provide personalised anticipation estimates. The present empirical
validation of Haflaga as the statistically grounded pattern provides a principled
basis for prioritising it in algorithmic implementations over patterns that do not
survive statistical scrutiny.

\subsection{Scope and Generalizability}

Before discussing specific limitations, we emphasise that all conclusions are
restricted to this specific dataset of non-Jewish NFP users. The results should
not be generalised to the general population or to observant Jewish women without
replication. The NFP dataset represents a self-selected group of cycle-aware women,
likely more regular than the general population, which may inflate pattern counts.
Any inference about the halachic validity of vesset rules in the Jewish context
requires datasets collected from the target population.
Such validation within an observant Jewish demographic would directly bridge the
statistical findings to their halachic application context.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several further limitations of the dataset and methodology are acknowledged, largely following
the systematic assessment in \cite{ross2022phd}.

\paragraph{Relabelling invariance ($H_k = L_k + 1$).}
The haflaga convention $H_k = L_k + 1$ is a relabelling that shifts all values
by one day. As a sensitivity check, we verified that all pattern counts are
identical whether computed on $H_k$ or directly on $L_k$ \emph{with the anchor
adjusted accordingly} (Week-Dilug at $L=29$ instead of $H=30$; emphasis added).
This anchor adjustment is critical: computing on $L_k$ with the unadjusted anchor
($L = 30$) yields only 12 Week-Dilug events instead of 18, which would be incorrect.
Provided the anchor is shifted by the same $-1$ that maps $H_k$ to $L_k$, the counts
are unchanged (Haflaga: 26, Week-Dilug: 18 under both conventions), confirming
that the $+1$ transformation is not a substantive modelling choice but a
conventional relabelling consistent with halachic convention~\cite{ross2021statistical,ross2022phd}.

\paragraph{Heaping and rounding.}
A potential concern is whether the distribution of haflaga values shows artificial
heaping at round numbers (e.g., $H = 28$ or $H = 30$) due to rounding or reporting
conventions in the NFP dataset. Note that the halachic haflaga convention $H_k = L_k + 1$ means the concern
is about heaping in the raw cycle lengths $L_k$: rounding at $L = 28$ (haflaga $H = 29$)
and $L = 30$ (haflaga $H = 31$). We examined the raw frequency distribution:
$L = 27$ ($H=28$, 194 cycles), $L = 28$ ($H=29$, 225 cycles, the mode),
$L = 29$ ($H=30$, 175 cycles, the Week-Dilug anchor), $L = 30$ ($H=31$, 141 cycles). The distribution peaks at $L = 28$, which reflects the
known biological mode of the menstrual cycle, and declines monotonically through $L = 30$.
There is no excess at $L = 28$ or $L = 30$ relative to neighbours, and no evidence
of rounding or reporting artefacts. The haflaga anchor value $H = 30$ (corresponding
to $L = 29$) similarly shows no excess (175 cycles vs.\ 225 at $H = 29$ and 141 at
$H = 31$). Figure~\ref{fig:heaping} confirms the smooth distribution.

\paragraph{Day-night timing (onset period).}
A central requirement of halachic vesset law is that a pattern may be established
only if all its constituent menstruations arrived during the \emph{same onset period}
--- either all during the daytime period or all during the nighttime period.  The NFP dataset records cycle length in whole days but not
the time of day of menstrual onset.  This creates two types of misclassification:
(i)~patterns that are counted as established in our analysis may not qualify
halachically because the onsets spanned both day and night; and (ii)~patterns that
are not counted may in fact qualify because the day-rounding convention used here
differs from the halachic one.  Both types of error introduce noise that attenuates
the observed pattern frequencies.  The net direction of misclassification bias is indeterminate without
data on onset timing: sequences counted as established patterns may not qualify
halachically (upward bias), while sequences that do qualify halachically may be
missed (downward bias). The two effects partially cancel, but their relative
magnitude cannot be assessed from the present data, and the limitation applies
equally to all five pattern types.

\paragraph{Calendar dates.}
The dataset does not record calendar dates, making it impossible to test the 10
date-dependent vesset types (e.g., \textit{vesset hachodesh}, the monthly calendar
anchor, and weekday-based patterns).  Only the 7 cycle-length-based vesset types
are accessible; of these, two (Dilug Chozer Chalila and Haflaga Chozer Chalila) had zero occurrences (mathematically: Dilug Chozer Chalila requires three consecutive
Dilug sequences, necessitating a minimum run of $\sim$9 cycles with a specific arithmetic
structure; given that a single Dilug occurs in only $\sim$4\% of possible triples in this
dataset, the expected count of three consecutive Dilug sequences is negligibly small;
the same reasoning applies to Haflaga Chozer Chalila). The two patterns actually
zero occurrences, leaving the 5 patterns analysed here.

\paragraph{Body-sign vesset.}
The dataset does not include prodromal physical symptoms (\textit{vesset haguf}, body-sign vesset: a category established when a woman
experiences a consistent recurring physical sensation --- such as yawning,
shivering, or lower-abdominal pain --- immediately preceding menstrual onset).  Women in the sample may have established body-sign
vesset patterns that are entirely invisible to a cycle-length analysis.

\paragraph{Population generalizability.}
The participants are non-Jewish NFP users \cite{fehring2013}: married women
aged 18--42 using natural family planning to avoid or achieve pregnancy.
The dataset excludes women in irregular hormonal states (menopausal,
perimenopausal, postpartum); inclusion required regularly cycling women of
reproductive age, so concerns about menopause or contraceptive washout are
not applicable. Several halachic authorities hold that regularities observed
in non-Jewish women may not generalise to Jewish women in practice
\cite{ross2022phd}.  More broadly, NFP users are a self-selected group who are
likely more cycle-aware and may have more regular cycles than the general population,
introducing a potential upward bias in regularity-dependent patterns such as Haflaga.



\paragraph{Null model structure.}
The permutation and multinomial null models shuffle cycle lengths globally across
women, ignoring within-person autocorrelation structure \cite{bortot2010,symul2019}.
The within-woman permutation null ($H_W$) addresses this by conditioning on each woman's
own cycle distribution and is therefore the more stringent test; future work could
also employ parametric nulls based on fitted state-space models \cite{bortot2010}.
Additionally, the within-woman permutation null assumes cycles within a woman are exchangeable (all orderings of her observed cycle-length multiset are equally probable under $H_0$), whereas a Markov chain model for cycle lengths would introduce temporal autocorrelation among successive cycles. If autocorrelation is present, certain orderings are more probable than others, and the permutation null overestimates the count expected under the true data-generating process; this makes $H_W$ conservative relative to a Markov null, so the non-significant $H_W$ results may partly reflect residual autocorrelation structure rather than the absence of pattern excess.

\paragraph{Pairwise dependence: cycle-count confound.}
The per-woman binary association analysis (Table~\ref{tab:chisq}) is subject to
a cycle-count confound: women with more cycles have more opportunities to
establish any pattern, potentially inflating co-occurrence rates independently
of the underlying process. Given the substantial variation in cycles per woman
(range 1--45), associations involving rare patterns (Week-Dilug: 18 women;
Dilug-in-Dilug: 23 women) should be interpreted with particular caution.
The two significant pairs (Haflaga $\times$ Week-Dilug and
Haflaga $\times$ Dilug-in-Dilug) are at least partly mathematically entailed
(set-theoretic inclusion and polynomial proximity), which provides independent
justification beyond the empirical association.

\paragraph{Sample size and power.}
The requirement for minimum run lengths (3 cycles for Haflaga and Dilug; 4 for
Dilug-in-Dilug) limits the number of testable windows, particularly for women with
few recorded cycles.  With a mean of 13.2 cycles per woman, longer patterns such as
Dilug-in-Dilug are likely underpowered.
To quantify this, we computed a minimum detectable count (MDC) for each pattern
under $H_W$ at 80\% power and $\alpha = .05$ (one-sided):
\begin{equation}
  \text{MDC} = \mu_W + (z_{0.05} + z_{0.80})\,\sigma_W
             = \mu_W + 2.487\,\sigma_W,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_W$ and $\sigma_W$ are the mean and standard deviation of the within-woman
permutation null distribution (Table~\ref{tab:power}).
All five observed counts fall below their respective MDC, confirming that the
current sample ($N = 118$ women, 1,554 cycles) is underpowered for individual
pattern detection under $H_W$.  Note that the joint Mahalanobis test under
$H_W$ is also non-significant ($D_M = 2.95$, $p = .121$), so underpowerment
applies at the multivariate level as well. The significant Mahalanobis test
($D_M = 5.10$, $p < .001$) reported in Section~\ref{sec:joint} refers to the
\emph{global} permutation null ($H_G$), not the within-woman null ($H_W$);
both results are genuine, but they answer different scientific questions:
$H_G$ tests overrepresentation relative to all women in the sample, while
$H_W$ tests whether temporal ordering within each woman's own cycles adds
overrepresentation beyond her individual cycle-length distribution.

\section{Conclusion}

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first formal statistical test of whether halachic vesset
patterns are overrepresented in empirical menstrual cycle data. The five patterns
analysed form a polynomial hierarchy whose structure generates mathematically
necessary dependencies, confirmed by pairwise randomisation-based dependence tests. Three complementary null
models were applied: a global permutation null, a multinomial null, and the
scientifically sharper within-woman permutation null ($H_W$).

The primary result is from the within-woman null: under $H_W$, \emph{no pattern is
individually significant} (all $p \geq .722$; joint $D_M = 2.95$, $p = .121$), and
all observed counts fall below their within-woman null means. These findings provide no evidence that
temporal ordering within each woman's cycle sequence creates vesset patterns
beyond what her own level of cycle regularity predicts.

Under the global null, Haflaga ($p < .001$) and Dilug ($p = .043$) are significantly
overrepresented, with a joint Mahalanobis test confirming significant displacement
from the null distribution ($D_M = 5.10$, $p < .001$). Only 2 of 50,000 permutation
simulations reached the observed Haflaga count. A focused joint test on the three
mathematically dependent patterns (Haflaga, Week-Dilug, Dilug-in-Dilug) is similarly
significant ($p < .01$). Together, these results establish that halachic vesset rules
function primarily as empirical detectors of individual cycle regularity: the rules
identify the subset of women whose cycles are intrinsically stable and formalise that
stability as an anticipatory pattern. The joint distance-based permutation test
introduced here is a methodological contribution with potential applications beyond
the halachic context. In particular, the Mahalanobis formulation offers a principled
approach for composite hypothesis testing when multiple correlated count outcomes
arise from shared biological structure: rather than applying multiple corrections
to individually underpowered tests, it aggregates evidence into a single joint
statistic that respects inter-pattern correlations. This may prove useful for
similar hypothesis-testing problems in cyclic biological processes more broadly.
