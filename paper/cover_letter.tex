Dear Editors,

\medskip

I am pleased to submit the manuscript \textbf{``Are Halachic Vesset Patterns
Overrepresented in Empirical Menstrual Cycle Data? A Permutation Test''} for
consideration as a Research Article in \textit{Biometrical Journal}.

\medskip
\textbf{What this paper does.}
Jewish religious law (\textit{Halacha}) prescribes that women anticipate menstruation
based on recurring patterns in their cycle history, collectively known as
\textit{vesset}. Despite their centuries-long practical application, no published study
has formally tested whether these patterns occur in real menstrual cycle data at rates
exceeding chance. This paper fills that gap using three complementary null models applied
to a publicly archived dataset of 1,554 cycles from 118 women (Fehring et al.,
\textit{Contraception}, 2013): a global permutation null ($H_G$), a multinomial sampling
null ($H_{iid}$), and --- as the primary scientific test --- a within-woman permutation
null ($H_W$) that conditions on each woman's observed cycle-length multiset.

\medskip
\textbf{Methodological contributions.}
The paper makes three methodological contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A permutation-based Mahalanobis joint test for dependent count vectors.}
    We introduce a distribution-free joint test ($D_M$) that evaluates whether a vector
    of sequentially computed pattern counts departs from the null distribution without
    assuming multivariate normality or independence among components. The test is applicable
    to any setting where multiple sequential pattern types are counted in time-series or
    longitudinal biological data.

  \item \textbf{A within-woman permutation null as a design-adapted exchangeability block.}
    $H_W$ permutes each woman's cycle-length sequence within her own record, conditioning on
    her personal cycle-length multiset. This block-randomization design controls for
    individual variation in cycle regularity and tests only whether temporal patterns exceed
    within-person chance levels --- a more targeted scientific question than the global null.

  \item \textbf{A polynomial hierarchy of pattern types.}
    The five vesset pattern types form a hierarchy based on the order of the
    finite-difference operator. We derive three mathematically necessary dependence
    relationships (set-theoretic inclusion, limiting convergence, logical incompatibility)
    and confirm each with pairwise Spearman correlation tests, motivating the joint test.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\textbf{Main findings.}
The primary result is that no vesset pattern is individually or jointly significant under
the within-woman null ($H_W$): all Holm-corrected individual $p \geq .198$ and the joint
Mahalanobis test is non-significant ($D_M = 2.95$, $p = .121$). This shows that observed
pattern counts are consistent with within-person randomness once each woman's personal
cycle-length distribution is controlled for, and provides the first formal permutation-based
test of halachic vesset patterns in empirical data.

Under the global permutation null ($H_G$), Haflaga departs significantly from chance
($z = 4.51$, $p < .001$; only 2 of 50,000 simulated datasets exceeded the observed count),
and the primary 5-pattern Mahalanobis joint test detects significant multivariate departure
($D_M = 5.10$, $p < .001$). The contrast between $H_G$ and $H_W$ is itself informative:
Haflaga overrepresentation relative to the population pool reflects partly the composition
of individual women's cycle-length distributions, not purely within-sequence patterning.

\medskip
\textbf{Fit with \textit{Biometrical Journal}.}
This paper fits \textit{Biometrical Journal}'s scope as a methodological contribution
motivated by a real biological problem. The central development --- a permutation-based
Mahalanobis joint test for correlated count vectors arising from sequential pattern
detection --- is a distribution-free alternative to Hotelling's $T^2$ with broad
applicability to cyclic biological settings (circadian rhythms, hormonal cycle data,
episodic clinical event sequences). The within-woman permutation null exemplifies
design-adapted exchangeability-block randomization (following Winkler et al., 2014;
Nichols et al., 2002) in a novel longitudinal context. The menstrual cycle application
provides concrete, publicly archived data for reproducibility.
